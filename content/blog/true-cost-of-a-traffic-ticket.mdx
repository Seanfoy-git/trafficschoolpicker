We combined fines and three-year insurance increases for all 51 US jurisdictions into one number: what a first speeding ticket (15 mph over, clean record) costs you over three years if you just pay it. The fine is the smaller part in every state. Insurance is at least 79% of the total everywhere.

**Correction, 26 November 2026.** Earlier versions of this study used insurance estimates from CarInsurance.com that ran far above other current sources. For Texas they came to nearly five times LendingTree's figure, and they put Michigan at $8,742. We've re-based every state on one source, LendingTree's September 2026 rate analysis. Every figure below, and on our state pages, uses the new basis.

## [Arizona](/arizona): the most expensive place to get a ticket, and the best place to fix one

A ticket for 15 mph over in Arizona costs $251 in fine and court costs (FinanceBuzz). LendingTree puts the three-year insurance increase at $3,428, the highest in the country. That's $3,679 all-in, about 15 times the fine.

Arizona is also where a course does the most. If you're eligible and complete Defensive Driving School, the court dismisses the citation and it stays off your Arizona driving record (A.R.S. § 28-3392), so your insurer has nothing to price. That keeps roughly $3,300 on our conservative count. You can use it once every 12 months.

At the other end, a [Vermont](/vermont) driver with the same ticket pays about $832 over the same three years. Same mistake, same clean record, and the state line is worth close to $2,850.

## The fine is the small part

The average fine for 15 mph over in America is $157 including court costs (FinanceBuzz, updated October 2025). LendingTree's national figure for one 11-to-15-mph-over ticket is a 23.8% insurance increase: $551.54 more a year, or $1,654.61 over three years.

Other current analyses land in the same place. ValuePenguin puts the average increase after a first speeding ticket at 24%, Bankrate at 23%, and Experian at 27%, or $582 a year. A ticket usually affects your rates for three to five years (Bankrate), so three years is the conservative case.

In [Washington](/washington) and [New Mexico](/new-mexico) the fine is under $60, and insurance is 98% of the cost.

## Where geography does the damage

The ten most expensive states are mostly in the West: Arizona, [California](/california), Washington, [Colorado](/colorado) and [Oregon](/oregon). [Louisiana](/louisiana), [Rhode Island](/rhode-island), [Michigan](/michigan), [Delaware](/delaware) and [Arkansas](/arkansas) make up the rest.

The order follows the insurance increase, not the fine. [Nevada](/nevada) has the highest fine in the country, $290 for 15 over, but a mid-table insurance increase, so it ranks 11th. [Florida](/florida) has the second-highest fine ($254) and still lands among the ten cheapest states, because its insurance increase is one of the smallest. [Texas](/texas) has the fourth-highest fine ($245) and ranks 41st of 51 overall.

New England splits sharply. Rhode Island is the fourth most expensive state. Vermont is the cheapest and [New Hampshire](/new-hampshire) the fourth cheapest.

## Where traffic school pays the most

A course saves you the insurance increase only where it keeps the ticket away from your insurer, so the savings track the increase. That's why the states where a ticket costs most are mostly the states where a course pays most.

Net savings below is the three-year increase avoided, less a flat $100 for the course and court fees. It leaves out any part of the fine a course also saves you, so it errs low. In Hawaii, Louisiana, Nevada, Oklahoma and Tennessee the saving depends on the court agreeing to dismiss the ticket.

Texas shows the arithmetic in full. Paying a 15-over ticket costs $245 in fine and court costs, plus about $1,013 in higher insurance over three years: $1,258. A driving safety course costs about $185 instead: $134 in court costs and state traffic fines you'd owe either way, up to $10 for the court's course fee, $12 for the certified driving record, and about $25 to $30 for the course. The ticket is dismissed (Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 45A.352), so it never reaches your insurer. That's about $1,070 kept, more than the table's ~$900, because the course also replaces the fine. It isn't available at 25 mph or more over the limit, or more than once in 12 months, and since Texas SB 296 (effective September 1, 2025) each citation from the same stop can be dismissed.

## The mechanics differ by state, and that's where drivers get tripped up

The same course can produce very different outcomes depending on where you live. Dismissal, masking, adjudication withheld, a base-rate discount, and a point-only credit are all different mechanisms with different effects on what your insurer actually sees. Don't assume a course guarantees dismissal or a discount.

- **[Arizona](/arizona) dismisses the citation** when an eligible driver completes Defensive Driving School (A.R.S. § 28-3392), once every 12 months.
- **[California](/california) masks the conviction** under Vehicle Code §1808.7, so the insurer typically never sees it. Available once every 18 months, only for a one-point violation in a non-commercial vehicle, and CDL holders are excluded.
- **[Texas](/texas) permits a dismissal** under Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 45A.352, generally once per stop in a 12-month window, and not for speeds 25 mph or more over the limit.
- **[Florida](/florida) withholds adjudication** when you timely elect a Basic Driver Improvement course (Statute §318.14). A separate statute prevents insurers from raising your premium solely because of that infraction.
- **[New York](/new-york)'s PIRP is a different animal.** It does not remove the conviction or points from the insurer-visible record. It gives you a mandatory 10% reduction on liability and collision base rates for three years, and subtracts up to four points for DMV suspension math only. Insurers still see the ticket.
- **[Michigan](/michigan)'s Basic Driver Improvement Course** keeps points and ticket info off the insurer-facing record, but only if you get an eligibility notice from Michigan SOS, finish an approved course within 60 days, and haven't used the benefit before. One time only.

Then there are the states where a course does less than drivers expect. [Pennsylvania](/pennsylvania) offers no elective course for a first speeding ticket; its Driver Improvement School only kicks in after you hit 6+ points, and even then it removes 4 points, not the conviction. [Massachusetts](/massachusetts) has no voluntary defensive-driving discount for a routine ticket. [New Jersey](/new-jersey) gives a 2-point credit once every five years and the conviction stays on your record. [Kansas](/kansas) has no statewide program, though local prosecutorial diversion in Wichita, Sedgwick County, and Shawnee County can dismiss eligible charges.

If you want the deeper decision framework, we broke it down in [traffic school vs paying the ticket](/blog/traffic-school-vs-paying-ticket) and [does traffic school remove points](/blog/does-traffic-school-remove-points).

## Speeding still kills 31 people a day

The money is one side of it. The other side is why these tickets exist at all. In 2024, speeding was a factor in 29% of all US traffic fatalities, killing 11,288 people, an average of 31 a day. That was the third straight annual improvement, with speeding deaths down 5.2% from 2023, and total US traffic fatalities dropping to 39,254, the lowest since 2020 (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 791, April 2026). It's still a leading contributor to fatal crashes, and the violation most drivers are likely to run into in a normal year.

## Methodology and sources

**Fines.** FinanceBuzz's figures for a first-offense ticket for 15 mph over, fine plus standard court costs, for all 51 jurisdictions (updated October 16, 2025). Local courts can charge more or less than the state figure.

**Insurance.** LendingTree's analysis of Quadrant Information Services rate data (September 2026; article updated November 2, 2026): the increase in a full-coverage premium after a first ticket for 11 to 15 mph over, for a 30-year-old man with a clean record driving a 2018 Honda CR-V with 50/100/50 liability limits. We use LendingTree's published three-year increase, rounded to the dollar. We chose 15 mph over for the fine so both halves describe the same ticket.

**Savings.** The three-year increase avoided, less a flat $100 for the course and court fees, rounded down to the nearest $50. Shown only for the ten states where the study records a course route that keeps the ticket away from insurers.

**Why the numbers changed.** Until November 2026 we used CarInsurance.com's Quadrant estimates for a 40-year-old with 100/300/100 limits. Different driver profiles produce different figures and different state orders. Hawaii is the clearest case: it was near the top of the old list and is second cheapest on LendingTree's. Treat rankings as source-dependent, and expect your own insurer's increase to differ with your carrier, age, tenure, discounts and history.
