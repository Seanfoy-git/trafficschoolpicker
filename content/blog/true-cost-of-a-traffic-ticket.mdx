We pulled together fines, three-year insurance surcharges, and traffic school rules for all 51 US jurisdictions and fused them into a single all-in number: what a first speeding ticket actually costs you. The fine is almost a rounding error. The insurance renewal is the real story. Here is what we found.

## In [Michigan](/michigan), a $135 ticket really costs $8,700

Start with the most extreme case in the country. A first speeding ticket in Michigan carries a base fine of about $135. The hidden bill is roughly $8,600 in insurance surcharges over the next three years. That puts the all-in cost at about $8,742, a 64x multiplier on the fine itself.

The math is stark. A Michigan driver's base rate runs about $3,964 a year. After one ticket it jumps to $6,833. That's $2,869 more per year for three years, or $8,607 in cumulative surcharge. The $135 fine was just the entrance ticket. The bill for admission was 64 times that.

For a [Vermont](/vermont) driver, the same behavior costs about $1,305 across the same three years. Same mistake, same clean prior record, and the state line is worth thousands.

## The fine is a rounding error

The average 10-mph-over fine in America is about $130. Put that next to the three-year insurance impact and it nearly disappears.

Recent industry analyses converge on a national average rate increase of about 24 to 25% after a single speeding ticket (Liberty Mutual, July 2026; ValuePenguin, June 2026), or roughly $435 more a year on a full-coverage policy per U.S. News (August 4, 2026). Nationally that works out to drivers with one ticket paying $2,503 a year versus $2,068 clean. Those increases last three to five years.

So the number on the citation is the visible cost. The insurance renewal is the cost nobody warns you about, and it's usually the one that hurts.

## Where geography does the damage

The most expensive states cluster in three zones. Michigan sits alone at the top. The Sun Belt and Southwest ([California](/california), [Texas](/texas), [Nevada](/nevada), [Arizona](/arizona), [Florida](/florida)) crowd the next tier. The Northeast corridor and Mid-Atlantic ([New Jersey](/new-jersey), [Delaware](/delaware), [Rhode Island](/rhode-island)) round out the worst offenders.

The cheapest states skew rural and low-premium: Vermont, [Montana](/montana), [Pennsylvania](/pennsylvania), [Nebraska](/nebraska), and [Ohio](/ohio).

The Northeast splits sharply. New Jersey is the fifth-most expensive state in the country to get a ticket. [New Hampshire](/new-hampshire) is the ninth-cheapest. Same region, very different renewals.

## Where traffic school pays the most

Here's the part most drivers never learn. The states where a course delivers the biggest payoff are the same states where the insurance hit is worst. Where a state-approved course keeps the ticket off the insurer-visible record, the savings track the surcharge you avoid.

Net savings below is the three-year surcharge avoided, minus a typical $25 to $100 course cost.

| # | State | Net savings from traffic school |
|---|-------|-------------------------------|
| 1 | [Michigan](/michigan) (Basic Driver Improvement masks points) | ~$8,600 |
| 2 | [California](/california) (masking under VC §1808.7) | ~$5,300 |
| 3 | [Hawaii](/hawaii) (court dismissal, case by case) | ~$5,050 |
| 4 | [Texas](/texas) (course dismissal, under 25 over) | ~$4,650 |
| 5 | [Louisiana](/louisiana) (court dismissal) | ~$4,300 |

| 7 | [Florida](/florida) (adjudication withheld, BDI) | ~$3,900 |
| 8 | [Tennessee](/tennessee) (driver improvement) | ~$2,950 |
| 9 | [Arizona](/arizona) (defensive driving, no points) | ~$2,950 |
| 10 | [Oklahoma](/oklahoma) (court dismissal + 2-pt credit) | ~$2,350 |

Texas is the cleanest example of the arbitrage. A driving-safety-course dismissal runs about $182 all-in once you include the roughly $144 court administrative fee and the $10 to $12 certified driving record, not just the $25 to $28 course tuition. That prevents an estimated $4,800 in three-year surcharges. Roughly a 26x return on a legal maneuver most drivers don't know exists. Note the mechanism is ticket dismissal, not a state-mandated discount, and Texas SB 296 (effective September 1, 2025) now lets multiple citations from the same stop each be dismissed.

## The mechanics differ by state, and that's where drivers get tripped up

The same course can produce very different outcomes depending on where you live. Dismissal, masking, adjudication withheld, a base-rate discount, and a point-only credit are all different mechanisms with different effects on what your insurer actually sees. Don't assume a course guarantees dismissal or a discount.

- **[California](/california) masks the conviction** under Vehicle Code §1808.7, so the insurer typically never sees it. Available once every 18 months, only for a one-point violation in a non-commercial vehicle, and CDL holders are excluded.
- **[Texas](/texas) permits a dismissal** under Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 45A.352, generally once per stop in a 12-month window, and not for speeds 25 mph or more over the limit.
- **[Florida](/florida) withholds adjudication** when you timely elect a Basic Driver Improvement course (Statute §318.14). A separate statute prevents insurers from raising your premium solely because of that infraction.
- **[New York](/new-york)'s PIRP is a different animal.** It does not remove the conviction or points from the insurer-visible record. It gives you a mandatory 10% reduction on liability and collision base rates for three years, and subtracts up to four points for DMV suspension math only. Insurers still see the ticket.
- **[Michigan](/michigan)'s Basic Driver Improvement Course** keeps points and ticket info off the insurer-facing record, but only if you get an eligibility notice from Michigan SOS, finish an approved course within 60 days, and haven't used the benefit before. One time only.

Then there are the states where a course does less than drivers expect. [Pennsylvania](/pennsylvania) offers no elective course for a first speeding ticket; its Driver Improvement School only kicks in after you hit 6+ points, and even then it removes 4 points, not the conviction. [Massachusetts](/massachusetts) has no voluntary defensive-driving discount for a routine ticket. New Jersey gives a 2-point credit once every five years and the conviction stays on your record. [Kansas](/kansas) has no statewide program, though local prosecutorial diversion in Wichita, Sedgwick County, and Shawnee County can dismiss eligible charges.

If you want the deeper decision framework, we broke it down in [traffic school vs paying the ticket](/blog/traffic-school-vs-paying-ticket) and [does traffic school remove points](/blog/does-traffic-school-remove-points).

## Speeding still kills 31 people a day

The money is one side of it. The other side is why these tickets exist at all. In 2024, speeding was a factor in 29% of all US traffic fatalities, killing 11,288 people, an average of 31 a day. That was the third straight annual improvement, with speeding deaths down 5.2% from 2023, and total US traffic fatalities dropping to 39,254, the lowest since 2020 (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 791, April 2026). It's still a leading contributor to fatal crashes, and the violation most drivers are likely to run into in a normal year.

## Methodology and sources

Fine data covers all 51 US jurisdictions for a first-offense 10-mph-over violation with no aggravating factors, total including court costs, as compiled by FinanceBuzz (updated October 2025). For the seven states without a statewide fine schedule, a named representative municipal or county schedule was used as the verified proxy.

Insurance surcharge data comes from CarInsurance.com, using Quadrant Information Services data (published June 25, 2026), for a 40-year-old driver with a clean prior record and full coverage at 100/300/100 limits. The three-year figure is the reported annual increase times three. Corroborating sources include Liberty Mutual, ValuePenguin, U.S. News, NerdWallet, Bankrate, Forbes Advisor, Experian, and The Zebra, which converge on a 24 to 25% national average increase. Where sources differ, we present ranges rather than single points.

One honest caveat on rankings: different studies use different driver profiles and produce different state orders, most notably for Hawaii, which LendingTree's 2026 study lists among the cheapest states while CarInsurance.com/Quadrant lists among the most expensive. Treat state rankings as source-dependent, not absolute. Individual outcomes vary by carrier, tenure, existing discounts, and prior history.
